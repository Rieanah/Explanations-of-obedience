Sit in the teacher's chair in a Milgram-style experiment, then explore the situational and dispositional explanations for why people obey.

Milgram (1963)

Aim: to find out how far ordinary people will obey an authority figure who orders them to hurt another person.

Method: 40 men aged 20 to 50 answered a newspaper advert and were paid $4.50 to take part in a "memory study" at Yale University. A rigged draw made each volunteer the teacher. The learner was an actor (a confederate). Each time the learner got a word pair wrong, the teacher gave a shock, rising in 15 V steps from 15 V to 450 V. A man in a grey lab coat (the experimenter) used four set prods if the teacher wanted to stop.

FindingResult
Reached 300 VAll 40 (100%)
Stopped at 300 V5 of 40 (12.5%)
Went all the way to 450 V26 of 40 (65%)
Signs of stressSweating, trembling, stuttering, nervous laughter; 3 had seizures

Conclusion: ordinary people obey authority far more than anyone predicted. Before the study, psychiatrists guessed only about 1 in 1000 would go to 450 V.

Situational explanations

These say obedience depends on the situation a person is in, not on what sort of person they are. Milgram tested this by changing one thing at a time. Tap a bar to see why it changed obedience.

Key terms

Proximity

How physically close the teacher is to the victim, or to the authority figure. The closer the victim, the harder it is to ignore their suffering. The further away the authority, the easier it is to disobey.

Milgram: learner in same room 40%. Hand on plate 30%. Orders by phone 20.5%.

Location

Yale is a respected university, so people trusted the study was safe and important. A run-down office carries less authority.

Milgram: office building 47.5%. Still high, which suggests location is not the only factor.

Uniform

A uniform signals legitimate authority. Milgram's lab coat made the experimenter look like an expert. When an ordinary person gave the orders, obedience fell sharply.

Milgram: ordinary clothes 20%. Bickman (1974): people were most likely to obey a stranger dressed as a security guard (about 80%) than one in smart civilian clothes (about 40%).

Extension ideas

Agentic state

Milgram suggested people see themselves as an agent carrying out someone else's wishes, so they pass responsibility up to the authority. In the autonomous state we take responsibility for our own actions.

Sign of it: teachers often asked "Who is responsible if he is hurt?" and relaxed when told the experimenter would take responsibility.

Legitimacy of authority

We obey people we see as having a rightful claim to give orders, such as a scientist at a university. Anything that undermines legitimacy (office building, ordinary clothes) cuts obedience.

Gradual commitment

The shocks rose in tiny 15 V steps. Having agreed to 15 V, it is hard to refuse the next small step, and so on up the scale. Stopping would mean admitting the earlier shocks were wrong.

Supporting evidence: Hofling (1966)

21 of 22 real nurses began giving a drug dose twice the maximum on a phone order from an unknown "doctor". This was a field study in a real hospital, so it supports the idea that obedience to authority is not just a lab effect.

Evaluating Milgram

Strengths
  • Controlled procedure, so it is replicable and the variations show cause and effect.
  • Many variations tested different situational factors.
  • Burger (2009) partly replicated it: 70% went past 150 V, which matches the original.
Weaknesses
  • Ethics: deception, psychological harm, pressure not to withdraw.
  • Lab setting and a strange task, so it may lack ecological validity.
  • Some teachers suspected the shocks were fake (Orne and Holland, 1968).
  • Only men took part, so results may not generalise.

Dispositional explanations

These say obedience depends on who the person is: their personality and character. Adorno (1950) proposed the authoritarian personality, a type of person who is more likely to obey and to look down on those of lower status.

Tap Next step to see how Adorno thought this personality develops.

Features of an authoritarian personality

  • Respects strict hierarchy and obeys those above them without question.
  • Sees the world in black and white, with little room for grey areas.
  • Hostile and contemptuous towards people seen as lower in status.
  • Rigid views, resistant to change, and tends to blame others.

Adorno measured this with the F-scale (the F stands for fascist tendencies).

Evidence for

Elms and Milgram (1966) interviewed 20 fully obedient and 20 defiant participants from the Milgram studies. The obedient group scored higher on the F-scale. They also described a stricter, more punishing relationship with their fathers.

Evidence against

  • The same kind of people behaved very differently when the situation changed: 65% obeyed in the baseline, 20% with ordinary clothes. Personality cannot explain that gap.
  • 65% of ordinary volunteers obeyed. They cannot all have an authoritarian personality.
  • The link is only a correlation, and childhood memories are not reliable.
  • Adorno's theory focused on right-wing views and ignores authoritarian people elsewhere.

Situational or dispositional?

Most psychologists now say both play a part. Situation seems to be the stronger force in Milgram's data, but personality may explain why some people in the same situation still refused.
